Production deploy exit 124 — root cause, proof, and the durable fix

A wedged SSHFS mount named in /etc/fstab made every systemd daemon-reload cost a hard 90.3 s. At ~63 reloads per deploy, that is ~95 minutes inside a timeout 30m envelope.

3 deploys burned Reproduced deterministically Fix measured: 90.42 s → 0.41 s Deploy GREEN under a wedged bridge PR #1591 + #1594 uaonvps · systemd 255

Final gate — passed. With the SSHFS bridge deliberately wedged in production (fuse daemon in T state, waiting=2, held for the whole run), a real deploy of PR #1594 ran green in 2 m 24 s, logging [reload-preflight] daemon-reload took 0s. That is the exact condition that produced exit 124 three times. The bridge was still wedged when the deploy finished, then the watchdog healed it autonomously: aborted fuse connection minor=60 → automount state=active.

1. The one-paragraph answer

systemd-fstab-generator runs on every systemctl daemon-reload, and for each /etc/fstab entry it canonicalizes the mount point — chase() → openat() → statx(). The desktop SSHFS bridge was an fstab entry, so when that mount is wedged (desktop asleep or unreachable) the statx() blocks in uninterruptible D state. The generator never exits; systemd kills its generator sandbox at the hard 90 s DEFAULT_TIMEOUT_USEC; the child exits non-zero and PID 1 logs Failed to fork off sandboxing environment for executing generators: Protocol error. Every reload then costs 90.3 s instead of ~0.5 s. A deploy performs ~63 reloads, so the installer block runs ~95 min and timeout 30m ssh kills it with exit 124.

The correction that matters. The suspicion going in was that the deploy triggers the automount and causes the wedge. It does not. Nothing in the entire deploy path reads /home/kjdragan — the only occurrence is a comment. The wedge is ambient (it starts and ends independently of deploys; on 08-01 it healed at 17:53:18 with no deploy and no unmount). The deploy's role is purely as an amplifier: it is the single biggest consumer of daemon-reload on the box.

“Protocol error” is a misnomer — fork did not fail. safe_fork(FORK_WAIT) converts a non-zero child exit status into -EPROTO. The sandbox child was created fine, ran, and exited 1 because its generators hit alarm(90). Chasing “why can’t systemd fork?” is a dead end — the message describes the messenger, not the fault.

A load-dependent problem produces a fat tail. This produces two disjoint clusters exactly 90 s apart — the signature of a fixed timeout. Arithmetically, slow = fast + exactly 90 s: the alarm(90) covers only generator execution, and the remaining ~300–1000 ms is the rest of the reload, which is precisely the fast population.

Two slow→fast transitions land exactly on SSHFS unmount timestamps, tying the two together before any experiment was run:

The 08-01 11:59→17:53 row is the one that disproves “mounted ⇒ slow”. The mount was up the whole time and reloads were fast for hours before and after. The trigger is the mount being wedged, not merely present.

O_PATH opens do not trigger FUSE; the next call on that descriptor — resolving the mount — does, and that is where it dies.

4. Proof the fix works

The bridge was converted to native systemd units and removed from /etc/fstab. Critically, the running mount at that moment had merely been adopted from the old generated unit — so I tore it down and let the repo unit create it from scratch before measuring. 5. Why the other four directions lost

(a) Self-defending deploy script — timeout 5 stat -t /home/kjdragan/. then lazy-unmount. Rejected: the probe is broken twice over.

It cannot time out. A hung FUSE call is uninterruptible: the child parks in D at request_wait_answer and timeout then blocks in sigsuspend waiting to reap a child that cannot die. SIGKILL is ignored. Measured still running at T+8 s on a 5 s timeout. The guard would hang the deploy harder than the bug.

It cannot tell the states apart. I hit this live: at the start of this session stat returned in 2 ms while the bridge was completely dead — because with the automount dead the path is an empty local directory. Healthy → 2 ms. Dead → 2 ms. Wedged → forever. The probe reports "fine" for two of the three states and hangs on the third.

And the remedy doesn't remedy. fusermount -uz / umount -l remove the mountinfo entry but leave waiting=1, the connection dir present, and the process in D.

(b) Mount-option tuning. Rejected — wrong layer. x-systemd.mount-timeout bounds a mount attempt; the block here is a statx() on a mount that is already up. x-systemd.device-timeout is a literal no-op (needs a What= that yields a .device unit; user@host:path yields none). x-systemd.idle-timeout is actively dangerous — periodically unmounting a possibly-wedged FUSE mount is exactly what killed the bridge on 07-31 and 08-01. And the hand-added ServerAliveInterval=15 was already on the box and demonstrably insufficient: wedges after it lasted 33 min and ~6 h, because the desktop's sshd answers keepalives even while the sftp request path is stalled. The declarative half of (b) survives — it is the same change as (c), and it shipped.

Dropping reconnect — the sharpest lever, deliberately not pulled. Without it a broken transport makes sshfs exit and subsequent syscalls fail instantly with -ENOTCONN, so reloads would also be fast. But reconnect is the only reason the bridge has ever healed itself in place (08-01 17:53:18). Trading the capability's self-healing for something we get for free by de-fstab-ing is a bad trade. It was only ever harmful because a generator was reading through it.

Bounding the reload (timeout 120 systemctl daemon-reload). Rejected — saves zero seconds. The D-Bus Reload method stashes the reply and returns; PID 1 does the work in its own loop. Killing the client cannot cancel PID 1, which stays single-threaded and D-Bus-unresponsive for the full 90 s — so the very next systemctl call just blocks for the remainder. It only looks like a fix in a log that times individual commands.

Raising the timeout envelope. Rejected. Surviving today's wedged reload count needs ~120 min, which parks the deploy-production concurrency group for two hours and serializes every subsequent merge behind a deploy that is doing nothing but waiting on a dead FUSE daemon. Converting a 30-minute failure into a 2-hour success is strictly worse.

(d) Batching the reloads — right idea, and the scoping matters enormously. The obvious version (collapse the ~20 explicit daemon-reload calls into one) is a no-op: a deploy actually performs ~63 reloads, because systemctl enable and disable reload implicitly unless given --no-reload. Batching only the explicit ones leaves 43 × 90.3 s ≈ 65 min — still over budget, with the fix appearing mysteriously ineffective. Correctly scoped it is real defense-in-depth; see §8 for why it is a separate PR.

Two things the incident taught that are now encoded

The IaC gap. The hand-added ServerAliveInterval=15,ServerAliveCountMax=3,reconnect existed nowhere in git — an exhaustive grep for fuse.sshfs, x-systemd.automount, allow_other, mount-timeout returned zero hits repo-wide. A VPS rebuild would have silently lost both the bridge and this fix. It is now repo-owned and reconverged every deploy. The doc that described it even asserted "there is no sshfs mount command anywhere in scripts/" — true when written, and precisely why nothing could see the drift.

Why "just unmount it" failed twice. pkill -f mount.fuse.sshfs does not kill a wedged sshfs daemon (it survives in futex_do_wait), and lazy unmount does not abort the connection. Both leave a non-empty unit cgroup — the journal's Found left-over process (mount.fuse.sshf) in control group — so systemd's next start fails with Result=resources and the bridge stays down rather than recovering. Only echo 1 > /sys/fs/fuse/connections/<minor>/abort frees D-state waiters. That is now the watchdog's first move.

Found while investigating: the bridge was already dead when this session started — home-kjdragan.automount had been inactive (dead) (Result: resources) since 00:57:24 UTC, roughly 80 minutes, with nobody aware. The documented Path Guarantee was silently gone. Restored, and the watchdog now catches this within 5 minutes.

7. Diagnose it in 30 seconds next time

ssh uaonvps 'bash -lc "grep -c fuse.sshfs /etc/fstab; \
  sudo journalctl _PID=1 --since -1h | grep -c \"Failed to fork off sandboxing environment\"; \
  time sudo systemctl daemon-reload"'

The fstab count must be 0 and a healthy reload is < 1.5 s. A non-zero EPROTO count plus a ~90 s reload means a generator is blocked on a wedged FUSE mount. This line now lives in project_docs/06_platform/04_deployment_and_cicd.md.

Exit 124 ≠ exit 255 — and the old runbook conflated them. The existing triage bullet described a slow daemon-reload deploy failure as the 255 case: connection dropped, deploy probably succeeded, run the SHA check. Applying that reflex to a 124 is exactly wrong — 124 means timeout 30m fired and the deploy genuinely did not finish. Now split into two bullets in 08_operations/05_incident_response_patterns.md.

8. Closing out the residual items — PR #1594

End-to-end proof: both original failure conditions, at once

The strongest single test. On the live box, the bridge line was re-added to /etc/fstab and the mount wedged (fuse waiting=3) — reproducing the incident exactly — then the deploy's own sequence was run against it:

StepResult
reload with the regression present90.41 s ← the bug, reproduced
bridge installer runsEVICTED /home/kjdragan from /etc/fstab
reload, same wedge still active0.44 s ← fixed
unwedge remediationdetected waiting>0, aborted conn, re-armed automount
bridge afterwardsfunctional, fstab clean, both units active

Added: deploy preflight self-heal

The deploy now times a reload before the installer block. If it exceeds 30 s, a generator is stalled anyway, so it runs the unwedge script and re-times. Worst case becomes one 90 s reload instead of ~63. Unlike rejected direction (a), both its probe (kernel waiting counter) and its remedy (connection abort) are measured to work.

Built, then rejected: batching 63 reloads → 1

I built the deferred-reload prelude and converted all 21 installers, then had it adversarially reviewed. It found six real regressions and I threw the work away:

  • Deferring the reload makes every in-installer systemctl start/restart act on a stale unit definition. mission_control_sweeper and autonomous_runtime are long-running services with no timer to re-fire — they would hold stale config indefinitely.
  • A failed VP worker degrades from a red deploy to a WARN line.
  • Every installer's own status output would report its timer as inactive — misleading in exactly the incident this tooling exists to diagnose.
  • The shared prelude was an untracked file sourced by 21 scripts; had it shipped unstaged, the || echo WARN wrappers would have made the fstab eviction itself silently skip.
  • It broke an existing test asserting the literal string systemctl enable --now.

The amplifier only bites when a generator stalls, and after the eviction /etc/fstab holds four entries, all local. Shipping six known regressions to defend a vector that no longer exists is a bad trade. The reasoning is recorded in the script, the doc, and a guard test so nobody re-attempts it blindly.
